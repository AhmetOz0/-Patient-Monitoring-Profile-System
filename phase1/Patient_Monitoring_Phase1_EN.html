Abstract

The Patient Monitoring Profile System is designed for a single hospital department to link patient records, timestamped health measurements, and staff task messages. Its relational model consists of six tables covering users, patients, current assignments, measurement types, measurements, and messages. Clinical access requires both an eligible user role and a current patient assignment. Each message has one sender, one recipient, and one completion result. This Phase 1 report defines the scenario, business rules, ER and UML models, table specifications, relational algebra queries, index design, transaction workflows, and evaluation plan. No implementation results or measured performance improvements are claimed.

CHAPTER 1 · INTRODUCTION

Project description and scenario

In a single department of a hospital, each patient’s measurement history and the tasks given for that patient are tracked in one shared record structure.

1.1 Background

In patient monitoring studies, delivering information into the clinician’s workflow is as important as collecting measurements. Electronic health record research identifies access control and data quality as common requirements. This project addresses these requirements in a small, department-level database design. [6] [11] [12] [16]

1.2 Project scenario

The department administrator registers the patient and assigns the doctor and nurse who will care for the patient. Assigned staff record the patient’s body temperature, pulse and blood pressure measurements with the date and time.

The doctor opens the patient profile and reviews the measurements. When needed, the doctor sends a task message about the patient to a nurse or another doctor assigned to the same patient. After completing the task, the recipient writes a result note; the message moves from Pending → Completed. The link between the patient, the measurements and the tasks is preserved.

1.3 Expected benefit and 1.4 problem statement

Measurements kept in separate lists and tasks passed on verbally make it hard to track which record belongs to which patient and who completed a task. The proposed system links this information through the patient; the recorder, the task recipient and the result are clearly visible. This is an expected process benefit; no measured clinical success or cost saving is claimed. [7] [8] [17]

1.5 Objectives

  • Store patient information and timestamped measurements consistently.
  • Restrict doctor and nurse access to the patients they are assigned to.
  • Send single-recipient tasks from a doctor to the relevant staff member and record the result.
  • Meet these needs with six tables, explicit keys and verifiable business rules.

1.6 Scope and limitations

The system covers a single hospital department. Measurements are entered manually; there are no patient accounts, appointments, prescriptions, billing, sensor connections, automatic diagnosis or emergency alarms. Notifications are shown in the in-app task inbox. Record correction history and a multi-department structure are not part of this design.

1.7 Proposed solution and roles

Table 1. User roles and project responsibilities
RoleResponsibility
Administrator · adminManages accounts, basic patient information and assignments. Has no access to clinical measurements or message contents.
Doctor · doctorReviews assigned patients and their measurements; adds measurements, sends tasks and completes tasks sent to them.

1.8 Development approach

The design was handled iteratively: the scenario and business rules were defined, entities were extracted, and the ER/UML models and relational schema were built. Any change in the rules must be reflected in the schema, queries and algorithms together.

1.9 Hardware and software selection

1.9.1 Hardware: A single personal computer is sufficient for development and the synthetic data experiment; no special medical device is used. The processor and memory of the computer used will be recorded in the experiment report.

1.9.2 Software: PostgreSQL was chosen for the database, Python/Flask for the server, HTML/CSS for the interface and draw.io for the diagrams. The browser communicates with the application server and never connects to the database directly. These are the technology choices of this design.

Design implications from the literature

The results of monitoring studies vary with the technology, patient group and care process. For this reason the project’s success will be evaluated through correct records, controlled access and a traceable task result, not on the assumption that it improves health outcomes. The measurement type/unit separation supports data quality, the patient–staff assignment supports the access boundary, and the single-recipient message supports task responsibility. [4] [10] [12] [14] [16] [19]

CHAPTER 3 · RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Method and design approach

3.1 Method

The patient, staff, assignment, measurement type, measurement and task message information in the scenario was modelled as separate responsibilities. Business rules were first written in natural language and then mapped to keys, multiplicities, constraints and transaction steps.

3.2 Need addressed

The need addressed in this project is tracking a patient’s measurement history together with the tasks given to staff, at the scale of a single department. It is not claimed that this need is unsolved in the literature; the contribution of the work is turning the selected narrow scope into a consistent relational design. [10] [15] [16]

3.3 Existing approaches and 3.4 comparison

Table 2. Design alternatives considered in the project
DecisionAlternativeChosen approach and reason
Patient–staff linkA single doctor field inside PatientsPatientStaff: allows more than one doctor/nurse to be assigned to the same patient.
Storing measurementsA separate column per type in the patient tableMeasurements + MeasurementTypes: stores repeated observations as dated rows with a shared unit.
Task resultSeparate tables for message, reply and notificationStatus and a single result inside Messages: sufficient for this project’s single-recipient task.
AccessRole check onlyRole + current patient assignment; plus a sender/recipient check for messages.

3.5 Project-specific transaction algorithm

The doctor’s patient selection, measurement review, recipient selection and task delivery are defined as one flow that ends with the recipient recording the result. The checks and state change are given in the transaction algorithms. This is transaction logic prepared for the project; it is not a new database engine algorithm or a scientific novelty claim.

3.6 Proposed system design

The business rules, six tables, ER/UML diagrams, algebra expressions and index plan below are parts of the same design. The patient profile is a screen that combines Patients with the related measurements and authorized messages; it is not a seventh table.

3.6.1 · DATA MODELING USING ENTITY

Business rules

The rules define the information in the scenario and the relationships between records. Numeric multiplicities are derived from these sentences. The writing style follows the given scenario and business rules example.

24 / 24 rules
  1. K01

    Every user has a user ID, username, full name, password hash, role and active flag. The user ID is the primary key; the username is also unique. The password itself is not stored.

    Related table →
  2. K02

    Every patient has an internal patient ID, a department patient record number, full name and birth date. The internal ID is the primary key; the patient record number is also unique. The birth date cannot be in the future.

    Related table →
  3. K03

    A user has exactly one of the roles admin, doctor or nurse. The role is set when the account is created and is not changed later. Administrators manage basic patient information and patient–staff assignments. Doctors and nurses are clinical staff.

    Related table →
  4. K04

    Patient information is recorded by staff. Patients are not given login accounts; patients and staff are different entities.

    Related table →
  5. K05

    A patient may have no clinical staff assigned, or one or more doctors or nurses. A doctor or nurse may be assigned to no patient, or to one or more patients.

    Related table →
  6. K06

    Each patient–staff assignment belongs to exactly one patient and exactly one staff member. The same patient–staff pair cannot be recorded more than once. The key of the assignment is made of these two IDs.

    Related table →
  7. K07

    Only active doctors and nurses can be assigned to patients. An administrator cannot be assigned to a patient as clinical staff. Only current assignments are kept in the assignment table.

    Related table →
  8. K08

    Doctors and nurses can view clinical profiles and add measurements only for patients assigned to them. Administrators cannot view clinical measurements or message contents.

    Related table →
  9. K09

    Every measurement type has a type ID, name and unit. The type ID is the primary key; the type name is also unique. This project uses body temperature (°C), pulse (bpm), systolic blood pressure (mmHg) and diastolic blood pressure (mmHg).

    Related table →
  10. K10

    For every measurement, the measurement ID, patient, type, numeric value, observation time, system record time and recording staff member are stored. Measurements are identified by their measurement ID.

    Related table →
  11. K11

    A patient may have no measurements or many measurements. Every measurement must belong to exactly one patient.

    Related table →
  12. K12

    A measurement type may not yet be used in any measurement, or may be used in many. Every measurement must belong to exactly one measurement type; its unit comes from that type.

    Related table →
  13. K13

    A clinical staff member may have recorded no measurements or many. Every measurement has exactly one recorder. That staff member must be active and assigned to the patient at the time of recording.

    Related table →
  14. K14

    For the four selected types, a measurement value must be positive, less than one million and have at most two decimal places. The observation time cannot be after the record time. The two blood pressure values are stored as two measurements of two different types.

    Related table →
  15. K15

    Recorded measurements are not modified or deleted within this scope. The name and unit of a measurement type that is already in use are not changed, so the meaning of past measurements is preserved.

    Related table →
  16. K16

    Every message has a message ID, related patient, sender, recipient, text, creation time, status, result note and completion time. Messages are identified by their message ID.

    Related table →
  17. K17

    A patient may have no messages or many messages. Every message must relate to exactly one patient.

    Related table →
  18. K18

    A doctor may have sent no messages or many. Every message has exactly one sender, and the sender must be a doctor. Nurses and administrators cannot send messages.

    Related table →
  19. K19

    A doctor or nurse may have received no messages or many. Every message has exactly one recipient. The sender and recipient must be different people and must both be actively assigned to the same patient at the time of sending.

    Related table →
  20. K20

    A new message is created in the Pending status; the result note and completion time are empty. The message text cannot be empty and can be at most 1,000 characters.

    Related table →
  21. K21

    Only the recipient, with an active account and a current patient assignment, can move a message to Completed. This requires a non-empty result note of 1–1,000 characters and a completion time. The completion time cannot be earlier than the creation time. A completed message cannot be completed again or returned to Pending.

    Related table →
  22. K22

    A message's content can be viewed by its sender or recipient while they have current access to the related patient. Other staff assigned to the same patient do not gain the right to see all messages because of that assignment.

    Related table →
  23. K23

    Patients, users and measurement types that have dependent records such as measurements or messages cannot be deleted. Staff accounts can be deactivated; past records are preserved.

    Related table →
  24. K24

    A staff member's account cannot be deactivated, and their assignment to the related patient cannot be removed, while a pending message addressed to them exists. The assignment can be removed after the tasks are completed. Removing an assignment does not delete past records; it ends current access.

    Related table →

K14 is a data format and record consistency rule; it does not define medical normal ranges. Role and assignment rules cannot be enforced by foreign keys alone; they are also checked in the transaction algorithms.

This choice matches the notation in the given VYS02 notes.

3.6.3 · TABLE DESIGN

Index design

Because the measurement history uses patient equality and a time range, and the task inbox uses a recipient/status filter and time ordering, four secondary B-tree indexes are planned. Column order was chosen for these queries. PostgreSQL multicolumn index documentation.

Transaction checks

In the pseudocode below, the user ID is taken from the session. Writes are done in a single transaction: they are rolled back if a check fails, and success is reported only after commit. The related users are protected with shared locks in ID order and the patient row with a write lock; in the completion operation the message row is locked last with a write lock. Assignment changes follow the same patient lock. Deactivation locks the user row for writing and checks pending tasks addressed to that user. In this way K24 stays consistent with task creation and completion.

A · Get patient profile

Input: session user u, patient p. Output: profile or access denied.

1. If u is not an active doctor/nurse, deny access.
2. If (p, u) is not in PatientStaff, deny access.
3. Get the basic information of patient p from Patients.
4. Filter Measurements by p; join with type and recording staff.
5. Return measurements in descending observed_at and measurement_id order.
6. Filter messages by p and (sender_id=u or recipient_id=u).
7. Show the profile with basic info, measurements and authorized messages.
B · Record measurement

Input: u, p, type t, value v, observation time. Output: new measurement or rejection.

1. Start transaction; take the related user and patient locks.
2. If u is not an active doctor/nurse or is not assigned to p, roll back.
3. Does t exist; is v positive, finite, within limits and at most 2 decimals?
4. Is the observation time after the record time? If invalid, roll back.
5. Insert p, t, u, v, observation and record time into Measurements.
6. Commit; return the new measurement ID.
C · Send task message

Input: sender u, patient p, recipient r, text. Output: Pending message or rejection.

1. Start transaction; lock users u and r in ID order,
   then lock patient p.
2. If u is not an active doctor or is not assigned to p, roll back.
3. If r is not an active doctor/nurse, not assigned to p, or r=u, roll back.
4. If the text is empty or longer than 1,000 characters, roll back.
5. Insert p, u, r, text, creation time and Pending into Messages.
   result_text=NULL; completed_at=NULL.
6. Commit; return the message ID.
D · Complete task

Input: session user u, message m, result text. Output: Completed or rejection.

1. Start transaction; lock user u.
2. Find message m and its patient; lock the patient, then the message row.
3. Is u an active doctor/nurse, the message recipient and assigned to the patient?
   If any condition fails, roll back.
4. If the status is not Pending or the result is empty/longer than 1,000, roll back.
5. Keep the recipient and Pending conditions in the UPDATE as well.
   status=Completed; result_text=result; completed_at=record time.
6. Commit if exactly one row changed; otherwise roll back.

If the same task is completed twice, only the first valid state change is accepted. Locking the patient row is a simple consistency choice for a small project; it serialises concurrent writes for the same patient.

When an unassigned patient and an invalid measurement type are sent in the same request, no record must be created. 3.11 Discussion of the design

The six tables cover the persistent information of the scenario and the eight foreign key relationships. A single-recipient message with the result kept in the same row keeps the scope small. Manual measurement entry, a single department, and the absence of assignment history and record correction history are the limits of the design. The experiment method is defined here; no implementation test or performance measurement is reported as if it had already been done.

Patient Monitoring Profile System — Phase 1 Project Report

Abstract

This work presents a relational database design for storing patient records, health measurements and staff-to-staff task messages in an integrated way in a hospital department. The design is based on six tables. Patient–staff assignments define the access boundary, measurement types standardise units, and the single-recipient message structure makes task responsibility explicit. Within Phase 1, the business rules, Crow’s Foot ER and UML models, table definitions, relational algebra expressions, index proposals, transaction algorithms and evaluation plan have been prepared.

1. Introduction

The Patient Monitoring Profile System is designed to store patient information and health measurements in an orderly way in a single department of a hospital, to allow authorised health staff to review them, and to send task messages about patients. The project links basic patient information with measurements recorded over time through the same patient profile. A doctor can review the past records of a patient under their responsibility and, when necessary, send a message to a nurse or another doctor assigned to the patient. When the recipient completes the task, they record a result note in the system and update the status of the message.

The users of the system have the roles administrator, doctor and nurse. Administrator accounts manage basic patient information and staff assignments. Access to clinical data by doctors and nurses depends on their role and, in addition, on being assigned to the related patient. No login account is defined for patients. Access to messages is also limited by the condition of being the sender or recipient, in addition to the patient assignment.

The core problem addressed is the difficulty of follow-up that arises when a patient’s measurement records and the tasks taken on by staff are kept separately. The aim of the project is to make the patient’s measurement history visible, identify who is responsible for a task and record its completion result. The scope is limited to a single department and manual measurement entry. Appointment, prescription, billing, sensor connection, automatic diagnosis and emergency alarm functions are not included in the design.

The solution is planned as a browser–application server–database structure. PostgreSQL was chosen for data storage, Python/Flask for application logic, HTML/CSS for the user interface and draw.io for modelling. No special medical hardware is required. Consistency between the scenario, business rules and models was handled with an iterative design approach.

2. Literature review

Twenty studies published between 2015 and 2026 were reviewed within the project. Remote patient monitoring reviews show that results can differ by patient group, technology used and care process. For this reason it was not assumed that a small database prototype would directly bring clinical improvement. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [17] [18] [19]

In health record studies, access control, data quality and usability stand out. In line with these findings, user accounts and patient information were separated; patient access was tied to role and current assignment; and measurement type and unit were kept in a central catalogue. Communication studies also support the importance of clearly defining responsibility and workflow. In the project this need is met with a single recipient, two message states and a mandatory completion note. [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [20]

3. Methodology and database design

Twenty-four business rules were defined from the scenario and turned into entities, keys, relationships and transaction checks. The core data integrity rules are that every patient has a unique record number, the same patient–staff assignment cannot be repeated, every measurement is linked to exactly one patient, type and recording staff member, and every message carries exactly one sender and one recipient. User roles are set when the account is created; the administrator has no access to clinical message and measurement content.

3.1 Roles of the six tables

The database design consists of six tables: Users, Patients, PatientStaff, MeasurementTypes, Measurements and Messages. Users stores staff accounts, Patients stores basic patient information, and PatientStaff stores the many-to-many relationship between patients and staff. The patient and user IDs in PatientStaff together form the primary key and prevent the same assignment from being recorded twice. MeasurementTypes holds measurement names and units, and Measurements holds the value, time and recording staff member of each observation. The Messages table keeps the patient link, sender, recipient and task result in the same record.

The measurement catalogue covers body temperature, pulse, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Each observation is stored in a separate row with a numeric value, observation time and record time. Messages start in the Pending status. The result text and completion time are empty at this stage. When the recipient enters a valid result, the status changes to Completed; a second completion is not accepted.

3.2 Crow’s Foot ER, UML and normalization

The ER model was prepared with Crow’s Foot notation. The double bar on the parent side of the eight relationships means exactly one record, and the circle and crow’s foot on the dependent side mean zero or many records. The Users–PatientStaff and Patients–PatientStaff relationships are identifying and are shown with solid lines; both foreign keys take part in the composite primary key of PatientStaff. In the other six relationships the foreign key is not in the dependent table’s primary key, so dashed lines are used. This distinction should not be confused with optional participation.

The UML class diagram shows the typed attributes and behaviours of the same six entities, and the multiplicities are kept as 1 and 0..*. Both models contain two separate links between Users and Messages for sender and recipient. Figure 1 presents the ER model and Figure 2 the UML model.

Patient and staff names are not repeated in transaction tables, and the measurement unit is obtained through MeasurementTypes. PatientStaff consists only of two key fields. Under the defined functional dependencies, partial and transitive dependencies were removed and the design was brought into third normal form. The patient profile is treated as a screen built by querying the existing tables; no separate profile table was added.

3.3 Relational algebra and indexes

Five core data needs were defined with relational algebra: the patients assigned to a staff member, a patient’s measurements in a time range, a recipient’s pending tasks, assigned patients without measurements, and the messages a user sent or received for a patient. Selection, projection, conditional join, set difference and union were used. Role and patient assignment are verified before clinical queries are executed.

Four secondary B-tree indexes are planned for patient–staff access, per-patient measurement history, the recipient/status task list and the per-patient message list. The first columns of the indexes were chosen for the equality filters in the queries, and the following columns for the time range and ordering. Indexes already provided by PK and uniqueness constraints were not proposed again. Extra storage and write cost were taken into account; no speed-up ratio is given without measurement.

3.4 Transaction algorithms

Transaction steps were prepared for viewing a patient profile, recording a measurement, sending a message and completing a task. Write operations are done in a single transaction and rolled back if a check fails. The user identity is determined from the session; role, active status and current patient assignment are checked. When a message is created, the sender must be a doctor and the recipient must be a different, active doctor or nurse assigned to the same patient.

A task can be completed only by the message recipient. The status check is also kept in the update condition, preventing the same task from being completed twice. User, patient and message locks are taken in a fixed order. While a pending task addressed to a staff member exists, deactivating their account or removing the related assignment is blocked. Parent records with past measurements and messages cannot be deleted.

3.5 Experiment and evaluation plan

Functional evaluation is planned with synthetic patient and staff records. Expected results are defined for correct assignment, unauthorised access, duplicate assignment, invalid measurement, unsuitable recipient and repeated completion scenarios. Multiple errors in the same request and the concurrent execution of assignment removal and message sending are compound error scenarios. No partial record is expected to remain after a failed transaction.

Query evaluation will use scales of 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 records; the same data and queries will be compared without and with the selected secondary indexes. Five warm-up runs and thirty measurement runs are planned for each query. Median time, p95, blocks read and index size will be recorded. The acceptance criteria are that result sets stay the same, the defined functional scenarios give correct results and unauthorised data access is blocked. At this stage no numerical results are presented as if these experiments had been carried out.

Evaluation and conclusion

At the end of Phase 1, a consistent six-table design was obtained for patient monitoring and task communication within the scope of a single department. Traceability was ensured between the business rules and the ER/UML models, table fields, relational algebra expressions, indexes and transaction algorithms. The limitations of the design are manual data entry and the absence of assignment history and record correction history. No clinical benefit, economic gain or measured performance success is claimed. The work presents the design and evaluation plan that will form the basis of the implementation.
